System Architecture

Propellants

Propellants were selected by the following considerations, in order of importance: practicality and availability, safety in handling, and finally, combustion properties.

Practicality and availability is of highest importance to us because of our limited ability to safely store and transport propellants. This immediately excludes all cryogenic propellants.

Limited access to safety training and equipment means a relatively non-toxic fuel that can be handled with simple eye and skin protection is favored.

Performance is of smallest importance because we are not as limited by weight due to the VTVL flight objective. Because of the previous requirements, as well as its heritage in amateur liquid propellant rocketry, Nitrous Oxide (N2O) is the obvious choice.

For fuel, by our previous criteria, our options are limited to petroleum fuels or alcohol fuels. The performance of each was evaluated using the NASA Chemical Equilibrium with Applications (CEA) tool. Using the Python package RocketCEA, the figure below was created.

Alcohol fuels were chosen for their performance at lower temperature, and Ethanol is our final selection for its slightly increased performance over IPA.

Pressure-Fed

Our propellants are fed by pressurizing the ullage above a piston with high pressure gas as seen on the left of the propellant tank figure. Both propellant tanks are pressurized this way. Pressurizing N2O ensures N2O will be a liquid in our feed lines and through our injector. This way, pressure drops due to throttle valves and line losses do not lead to a two-phase flow and therefore the density is only a function of pressure and temperature.

Component Design

Throttle Valves

VTVL presupposes the ability to throttle the liquid propellant engine. Several studies have done tests on variable area cavitating venturi (VACV) valves to control mass flow rate of propellants. Also, VACVs act as both valves and dampers of propellant system vibrations. Our VACVs are designed from the paper Application of variable area cavitating venturi as a dynamic flow controller by Tian et al. The design allows for the mass flow of the entire feed line to be controlled by one valve as well as allowing for mass flow to vary nearly linearly with pintle stroke.

To validate our designs before manufacturing, I did a mixture analysis using ANSYS Fluent and structural analysis using ANSYS Mechanical and COMSOL.

The results of the simulation show cavitation, but present surprising evidence showing that flow is controlled in the diverging section, and not the throat. I am still resolving issues in the simulation to determine what the range of ΔPs is that cause a constant mass flow. I am also considering geometries with longer throats.

Propellant Tanks

Propellant tanks were designed using Half Cat Rocketry's pressure vessel design guide, Half Cat Sim, and the Parker O-Ring Handbook.

The most delicate part of the tank design is the bulkhead. Due to the large diameter, excess thickness contributes significantly to weight. I used ANSYS Mechanical to determine what thickness the bulkhead can be. To seal the bulkhead, I followed the Parker O-Ring handbook recommendations for static seals. O-Ring material compatibility was also considered. Viton or Buna-N were selected based on availability, with Viton preferred whenever available.

Additionally, I created engineering drawings for all four bulkheads (see below).

Air and Servo Actuated Ball Valves

Our larger propellant on/off valves require torque that reasonably sized servo motors cannot provide. Instead, air actuated on/off valves were selected. Off-the-shelf solutions were not found to be satisfactory. I designed a simple mount to connect our ball valves and air actuators such that it could be easily machined by basic lathe and vertical mill operations.

Propellant Tank Mounts

In order to mount our propellant tanks to the super structure, a mounting system that is strong, but allowed for adjustment was needed. To suit this need, I designed mounts cut from billet aluminum and rails bent from aluminum sheet.

3. Independent Research: Multiphase CFD of Flashing Nitrous Oxide

Skills developed: two-phase and metastable flow theory, finite-volume CFD (OpenFOAM-12), COMSOL Multiphysics, scripted meshing (gmsh), building and running software on HPC with MPI, boundary-condition design, numerical debugging, and technical writing.

Background: N2O has a high saturation pressure at room temperature. When the pressure downstream of an injector, or other constriction, drops below the saturation pressure the liquid begins to flash. Its density changes along the flow path, and the standard orifice equations no longer predict mass flow rate. Halo's cavitating venturis and flow-controlling injectors like the ones on Altaris and Ortus rely on this behavior. Accurately predicting mass flow is therefore central to both component design and engine performance.

Goal: Build a CFD code that predicts the mass flow of flashing N2O through arbitrary millimeter-scale geometries, then compare it against published injector data. The work is partially advised by Prof. Eric Matthys and documented, including failed approaches, in a 32-page report (May 2026).

Literature Review: Existing Mass Flow Models

Before simulating, I reviewed the analytical and semi-empirical models summarized in Waxman's (Stanford, 2014) thesis. These models bound what a physical simulation should predict.

Flashing-flow models reviewed.
ModelKey assumption
Single-phase incompressible (SPI)Liquid stays liquid; ṁ = Cd A √(2ρΔP). Over-predicts flow when flashing occurs.
Homogeneous equilibrium (HEM)Phases are in thermodynamic equilibrium with no slip, and expansion is isentropic. Under-predicts flow.
BurnellNon-equilibrium; an empirical coefficient accounts for liquid superheat (metastability).
Dyer et al.Blends SPI and HEM using a parameter κ that reflects finite bubble growth time.

The core difficulty is metastability. Liquid N2O can remain liquid inside the saturation dome for some distance before it flashes, which is highly unpredictable.

Tool Selection: COMSOL to OpenFOAM

I first learned COMSOL from scratch, working through single-phase laminar and turbulent studies to build intuition for convergence and boundary conditions. COMSOL has no built-in cavitation or flashing model, so I moved to OpenFOAM, which uses the inherently conservative finite-volume method. It also offers two relevant multiphase modules: compressibleVoF, which uses the Schnerr–Sauer cavitation model, and multiphaseEuler, which solves a separate velocity field for each phase.

HPC Environment

I compiled OpenFOAM-12 from source on UCSB's GRIT HPC cluster and configured MPI domain decomposition. Later runs took 6–14 hours each on this setup.

Meshing

OpenFOAM's built-in meshers give no visual feedback, so I wrote geometry and refinement scripts in gmsh. Because the finite-volume method needs cells with volume, each 2-D mesh is a single cell-thick extrusion with empty front and back patches. I also attempted an axisymmetric wedge, but gmsh consistently generated a degenerate zero-area surface on the axis, so I used the planar 2-D domain instead. The final meshes ranged from 64k to 397k cells.

Mesh refinement vs. time step. Refining the mesh to fix instabilities, a habit carried over from COMSOL, made them worse. For a fixed maximum Courant number Co = uΔt/Δx, the smallest, fastest cells set the global time step so the refined inlet hit the Courant limit significantly before the rest of the domain.

Outlet backflow. Vortices shed from the channel exit diffused normally until they reached the outlet boundary where they caused reverse flow and a blowup in velocity. The domain must be long enough for turbulence to decay significantly before the flow reaches the boundary, and the boundary condition has to suit high-speed flow, where the dynamic pressure is large.

Discretization. I use bounded Total Variation Diminishing (TVD) schemes: vanLeer for phase fraction, which jumps sharply between 0 and 1, and limitedLinear for most other advected fields. These suppress the overshoots that higher-order schemes produce at steep gradients.

Next Steps

Next, I plan to slowly ramp the outlet pressure over many seconds to soften the phase change computing cost. After that, I will compare predicted mass flow against the SPI, HEM, and Dyer bounds and Waxman's measured injector data to check for a physical result. Finally, I will apply the workflow to Halo's cavitating venturi, where earlier Fluent results suggested that flow is controlled by flow in the diverging section.
